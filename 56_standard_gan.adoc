== Introduction ==
GAN is a neural network based implicit density modelling generative model. It models the input data distribution stem:[p_{\text{data}}] implicitly (there is no parametric modelling of the input distribution). The result is a sampler that draw high quality samples from stem:[p_g] which is a distribution that is modelled by GAN. After training, stem:[p_g] will be a good approximation to the unknown stem:[p_{\text{data}}] distribution.

The model architecture consists of a generator and a discriminator which learn through competition with each other. It is a framework for estimating generative models via an adversarial process, in which we simultaneously train two models:

* A generative model stem:[G] that captures the data distribution
* A discriminator model stem:[D] that estimates the probability that a sample came from the training data rather than stem:[G].

The training procedure for stem:[G] is to maximize the probability of stem:[D] making a mistake.

== Overview ==
The GAN consists of two neural networks: a discriminator and a generator. The generator takes random noise as input and generate fake data that is as close as possible to the real data. The discriminator receives fake data generated by the generator or real data as input and distinguishes whether the input is real or fake.

The discriminator uses a sigmoid activation function to output values between 0 and 1. When real data is input, the discriminator outputs a value closer to 1, and when fake data is input, the discriminator outputs a value closer to 0.

image::.\images\standard_gan_01.png[align='center', 400, 400]

If the GAN model is well trained, the discriminator can distinguish between real data and fake data, and the generator can produce fake data that is so similar to real data that the discriminator cannot distinguish between them. The discriminator and generator are in competition with each other, and gradually reach a Nash equilibrium as the model is trained. When in equilibrium, the discriminator outputs 0.5 when real data is input and also outputs 0.5 when fake data is input. That is, the fake data becomes so similar to the real data. As a result, the generator can produce fake data that is very similar to real data.

After training is complete, we can use the generator to produce an infinite amount of fake data that looks similar to real data.

== Loss Function for Training ==
Training a GAN requires two objective functions: one for training the discriminator, and one for training the generator. The two objectives work together. Some notations:

* Let stem:[G] be a generator and stem:[D] be a discriminator. And let's call the parameter set of stem:[D] as stem:[\boldsymbol{\theta}_D], and the parameter set of stem:[G] as stem:[\boldsymbol{\theta}_G].

* Let stem:[\mathbf{x}] be the real data from the distribution stem:[p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})]. And let stem:[\mathbf{z}] be the random noise coming from some distribution stem:[p_z(\mathbf{z})], such as an uniform distribution or a normal distribution.

* When stem:[\mathbf{x}] is input to stem:[D], let stem:[D(\mathbf{x})] be the output of the discriminator. The discriminator outputs the probability that the input is real. When stem:[\mathbf{z}] is input to stem:[G], let stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] be the output of the generator. The generator samples stem:[\mathbf{z}] from the latent space (random noise) and maps it to the input data space. The output of the generator forms a distribution stem:[p_g(\mathbf{x})] over the data space. 

====
Our main objectives are that

* We want the discriminator output close to 1 for fake data points, i.e., stem:[D(G(\mathbf{z})) = 1]. We should update the parameters of the generator for this to happen. Train the stem:[G] such that stem:[D] misclassifies the generated fake sample into class 1.

* We want the discriminator to output 1 for real data points and 0 for fake data points, i.e., stem:[D(G(\mathbf{z})) = 0] and stem:[D(\mathbf{x}) = 1]. We should update the parameters of the discriminator for this to happen.

And ultimately, we want stem:[p_g(\mathbf{x}) \approx p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})].
====

Let stem:[V(D, G)] be the loss function for training a GAN model.

[stem]
++++
V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}[\log D(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z} \sim p_z(\mathbf{z})}[\log (1- D (G(\mathbf{z})))]
++++

* Objective for discriminator training: stem:[\max_{D} V(D, G)]. The discriminator tries to maximize the stem:[V(D,G)] by making stem:[D(\mathbf{x})] equal to 1, and stem:[D(G(\mathbf{z}))] equal to 0. That is, when real data stem:[\mathbf{x}] is input, the discriminator updates stem:[\boldsymbol{\theta}_D] so that the output stem:[D(\mathbf{x})] has a value close to 1. And when fake data stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] is input, it updates stem:[\boldsymbol{\theta}_D] so that the output stem:[D(G(\mathbf{z}))] has a value close to 0.

* Objective for generator training: stem:[\min_{G} V(D, G)]. The generator tries to minimize the stem:[V(D,G)] by making stem:[D(G(\mathbf{z}))] equal to 1. That is, when fake data stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] is input to the discriminator, the generator updates stem:[\boldsymbol{\theta}_G] so that the output stem:[D(G(\mathbf{z}))] of the discriminator has a value close to 1.

NOTE: The generator doesn't directly affect the stem:[\log D(\mathbf{x})] term in stem:[V(G,D)].

image::.\images\standard_gan_02.png[align='center', 400, 400]

Once training is complete, the generator receives random noise stem:[\mathbf{z}] and generates stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] that resembles real data. The discriminator cannot tell whether the input stem:[\mathbf{x}] or stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] is real or fake. That is, both stem:[D(\mathbf{x})] and stem:[D(G(\mathbf{z}))] are 0.5. This means that stem:[D(\mathbf{x})] is identical to stem:[D(G(\mathbf{z}))].

So the minmax objective for GAN training is:

[stem]
++++
\min_{G} \max_{D} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}[\log D(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z} \sim p_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z})}[\log (1- D (G(\mathbf{z})))]
++++

* The objective of the discriminator is to make the stem:[D(\mathbf{x})=1] and stem:[D (G(\mathbf{z})) = 0]. So, the terms will stem:[\log 1 + \log 1 = 0]. The discriminator tries to make the loss close to 0 (maximizing it).

* The objective of the generator is to make the stem:[D (G(\mathbf{z})) =1]. So, the second term will be stem:[\log 0 = -\infty] or undefined. The generator tries to make the loss tends towards stem:[-\infty] (minimizing it).

== Learning Algorithm of GAN ==
The algorithm alternately updates the network parameters of the discriminator and generator.

. Sample minibatch of stem:[m] noise samples stem:[\{\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(m)}\}] from noise prior stem:[p_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z})]. stem:[\mathbf{z}] can be of any dimension, typically low dimension than the input data stem:[\mathbf{x}]. But the output of the generator stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] should be of the same dimension as stem:[\mathbf{x}].
. Sample minibatch of stem:[m] examples stem:[\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{x}^{(m)}\}] from data generating distribution stem:[p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})].
. Freeze the generator parameters. The objective of the disciminator is to maximize the objective function:
+
[stem]
++++
\max_{D} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}[\log D(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z} \sim p_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z})}[\log (1- D (G(\mathbf{z})))]
++++
+
The discriminator loss for the mini-batch is computed as:
+
[stem]
++++
\mathcal{L}_D = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left[ \log D(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) + \log (1- D (G(\mathbf{z}^{(i)}))) \right]
++++
+
[NOTE]
====
Implicitly, it is equivalent to considering the target label stem:[y] as 1 for the real data stem:[\mathbf{x}] and 0 for the fake data stem:[G(\mathbf{z})].

[stem]
++++
\mathcal{L}_D = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left[ y \log D(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) + (1-y) \log (1- D (G(\mathbf{z}^{(i)}))) \right]
++++
====
+
The discriminator aims to maximize this function stem:[\mathcal{L}_D]: it wants stem:[D(\mathbf{x}) \to 1] and stem:[D (G(\mathbf{z})) \to 0] (the result stem:[\mathcal{L}_D ] will be close to 0). We compute the gradient with respect to the discriminator parameters stem:[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_D}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}_D}]. This is computed via backpropagation. Once the gradient is computed, we use gradient ascent to update the parameters:
+
[stem]
++++
\boldsymbol{\theta}_D \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}_D + \eta \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_D}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}_D}
++++

. Steps 1,2 and 3 are repeated stem:[k] times, i.e., stem:[\boldsymbol{\theta}_D] is updated stem:[k] times, where stem:[k] is a hyperparameter.
. Sample minibatch of stem:[m] noise samples stem:[\{\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(m)}\}] from noise prior stem:[p_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z})].
. Freeze the discriminator parameters. The objective of the generator is to minimize the second term (as the first term is independent of stem:[G]) by keeping the discriminator parameters stem:[\boldsymbol{\theta}_D] fixed:
+
[stem]
++++
\min_{G} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z} \sim p_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z})}[\log (1- D^* (G(\mathbf{z})))]
++++
+
In the early stage of training, the discriminator can easily detect fake samples, i.e., we get stem:[D^* (G(\mathbf{z})) = 0]. This leads to stem:[\log (1-D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))) \approx \log (1-0) = 0]. In such cases, the gradient with respect to the generator parameters becomes very small (near-zero slope). This means the generator receives weak gradients, which slows or stalls learning.
+
[stem]
++++
\frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}_G} \log (1- D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))) = \frac{-1}{1- D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))} \frac{\partial D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}_G}
++++
+
So the author of the paper proposed a heuristic, but very effective alternative. Instead of minimizing stem:[\log (1- D^* (G(\mathbf{z})))], we maximize stem:[\log D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))]. Equivalently, we minimize stem:[- \log D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))]. This is known as the non-saturating loss for the generator.
+
[stem]
++++
\frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}_G} - \log D^* (G(\mathbf{z})) = \frac{-1}{D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))} \frac{\partial D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}_G}. 
++++
+
The non-saturating loss provides larger gradients when stem:[D^* (G(\mathbf{z}))] is small. So the generator learns faster and avoids early stagnation.
+
So the generator loss for the mini-batch is computed as:
+
[stem]
++++
\mathcal{L}_G = - \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \log D^* (G(\mathbf{z}^{(i)}))
++++
+
[NOTE]
====
Implicitly, it is equivalent to considering the target label stem:[y] for the fake data as 1 as we are trying to fool the discriminator. But note, we are not updating the disciminator parameters here.
====
+
The generator tries to minimize this function stem:[\mathcal{L}_G]. We compute the gradients and update the generator parameters through gradient descent:
+
[stem]
++++
\boldsymbol{\theta}_G \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}_G - \eta \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_G}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}_G}
++++

This is one training iteration (one epoch). Note we update the discriminator parameters stem:[k] times and the generator parameters only once. In the training, we use alternating optimization strategy. That is, we freeze the generator parameters when training the discriminator. And freeze the discriminator parameters when training the generator. This is essential to ensure that the generator learns how to adjust its own parameters to better fool the current state of the discriminator.

*When do we stop the training?*

After many iterations, the generated samples look close to the real samples. Then, the discriminator will be receiving a bunch of real inputs with label 1 and a bunch of fake (with similar patterns as in the real data) inputs with label 0. Looking at the same pattern with two different labels 0 and 1, the discriminator gets confused and it outputs a probability close to 0.5. The classifier is not confident in discriminating real versus fake.

Thus, when the discriminator outputs consistently 0.5 for the all real and fake data points, we stop the training process and say the training is converged.

== Changes in Data Distribution ==
Let's look at how the distribution of fake data generated by the generator and the distribution of real data change as stem:[D] and stem:[G] are updated. Note stem:[D] is the output of the discriminator and stem:[G] is the output of the generator.

. (a) is an initial state. The distribution of real data stem:[\mathbf{x}] is stem:[p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})]. The distribution of random noise is stem:[p_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z})] - assume an uniform distribution. The output of the generator is stem:[G(\mathbf{z})]. Let the distribution of the fake data stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] be stem:[p_g(\mathbf{x})]. Let's assume that stem:[p_g] is somewhat similar to stem:[p_{\text{data}}] and stem:[D] is a partially accurate classifier in this state.

. (b) In the inner loop of the algorithm, stem:[D] is updated (as the result of updating the parameters of the discriminator) stem:[k] times to converge to stem:[D^*].
** When stem:[\mathbf{x}] from stem:[p_{\text{data}}] is input to the discriminator, stem:[D^*(\mathbf{x})] becomes 1. 
** When stem:[\mathbf{x}] from stem:[p_g] is input to the discriminator, stem:[D^*(\mathbf{x})] becomes 0.
** When stem:[\mathbf{x}] from the overlapping part of the two distributions is input to the discriminator, stem:[D^*(\mathbf{x})] will have a value between 0 and 1. In this case, the discriminator cannot accurately distinguish whether stem:[\mathbf{x}] is real or fake.

. (c) stem:[G] is updated once. stem:[p_g] and stem:[p_{\text{data}}] become more similar in this state.
. (d) Steps (b) and (c) are repeated multiple times. After learning is complete, the distributions of stem:[p_g] and stem:[p_{\text{data}}] match well. And both stem:[D^*(\mathbf{x})] and stem:[D^*(G(\mathbf{z}))] become 0.5.
+
That is, the discriminator cannot distinguish between stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] generated by the generator and real data stem:[\mathbf{x}], which means stem:[G(\mathbf{z})] is very similar to stem:[\mathbf{x}].
